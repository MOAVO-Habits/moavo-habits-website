# 7 Brain Chemicals Explained Like a Building: Dopamine, Serotonin, GABA, and More

Lately, I've been reading a Korean book called *뇌를 이기는 습관*, which roughly translates to "Habits That Beat Your Brain." It uses brain science to explain why our habits are so hard to change.

As I read, the same words kept coming up:

Dopamine, serotonin, adrenaline, noradrenaline, glutamate, acetylcholine, GABA.

Honestly, I sort of understood what they meant, but none of it stuck. Even the names sound like they belong in a medical textbook.

So I decided to sort it out my own way. I studied spatial design in college and worked in the field for almost 10 years. Whenever I run into a hard concept, I can't help turning it into a **building** in my head.

For example, I often picture the human body like this:

* *Bones = steel frame and rebar*
* *Muscles = concrete*
* *Blood vessels = plumbing*
* *Nerves = electrical and communication lines*
* *Skin = finishes and interior*
* *Brain = the building's control room*

So today, let's think of the brain as a building,

and the 7 brain chemicals as the **systems** inside it.

## 1. The brain is a building with 7 key systems

For a building to run well, every system has to do its job: power, lighting, heating and cooling, fire alarms.

The brain is the same. Brain chemicals are what let brain cells send signals to each other, and each one has its own job. Let's go through them one at a time.

**① Glutamate: the main power switch**

It turns the brain on and gets it going. Think of it as the main switch that sends power through the building.

Whenever you think, learn, or remember something, glutamate is what "switches the brain on."

**② GABA: the circuit breaker**

It calms the brain down and switches it off. It's the circuit breaker that trips when too much current is flowing, so nothing overheats.

It helps your body loosen up and your mind settle down.

**③ Dopamine: the neon sign**

Dopamine creates that powerful feeling of "I want that" and the drive to go get it.

It's the bright neon sign you can spot from a block away, the one that pulls you in. It's the engine that gets us moving.

**④ Serotonin: the thermostat**

Serotonin gives you that sense of relief and calm: "Okay... this is good enough."

Think of it as the thermostat that keeps the inside of the building comfortable, not too hot and not too cold. When serotonin runs low, we tend to get touchy over small things and become more defensive.

**⑤ Adrenaline: the fire alarm**

In a crisis, adrenaline gets your body moving right away. The moment you sense danger, your whole body tenses up.

It's like the fire alarm that goes off through the entire building. It makes your heart race and your blood pressure rise so your body can react fast.

**⑥ Noradrenaline: the security alert switch in the control room**

Noradrenaline makes you sharp and alert, and when the situation changes, it pushes you to change your approach.

Think of it as the switch in the building's control room that says, "We're on high alert, starting now!" If adrenaline wakes up your body, noradrenaline wakes up your attention.

**⑦ Acetylcholine: the task light**

Acetylcholine helps you focus and learn, and it also helps your muscles move.

It's the task light that lights up your desk when you're focused on detailed work. It's essential whenever you're learning something new.

Here are all 7 at a glance:

| Brain chemical    | Building system       | What it does                          |
| ----------------- | --------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| **Glutamate**     | Main power switch     | Turns the brain on and gets it going  |
| **GABA**          | Circuit breaker       | Calms the brain and switches it off   |
| **Dopamine**      | Neon sign             | Creates strong anticipation and drive |
| **Serotonin**     | Thermostat            | Brings a sense of calm and stability  |
| **Adrenaline**    | Fire alarm            | Gets the body moving in a crisis      |
| **Noradrenaline** | Security alert switch | Raises alertness and adapts to change |
| **Acetylcholine** | Task light            | Helps you focus, work, and learn      |

*The real brain is far more complicated than this. One chemical doesn't control one emotion. Many work together at once. The building metaphor here is simplified to make things easier to understand.*

## 2. They're easier to remember in pairs

You don't have to memorize all 7. Group them into three pairs, and it gets much easier.

**1. On vs. off: glutamate and GABA**

They're the gas pedal and the brake. Your brain runs on the balance between the two.

With only the gas pedal (glutamate), you overheat. With only the brake (GABA), you can't move.

**2. Wanting vs. feeling satisfied: dopamine and serotonin**

Dopamine shouts, "More! More! One more time!" Serotonin says, "It's okay. This is enough."

You absolutely need both: drive (dopamine) and calm (serotonin).

**3. Body vs. mind: adrenaline and noradrenaline**

As their names suggest, they're practically brothers.

Adrenaline mainly makes your heart pound,

while noradrenaline sharpens your alertness in the brain.

## 3. Same systems, different buildings: reactivity

Every building has similar systems, but **how sensitively those systems react is different in every building.** In some buildings, a little smoke sets off the alarm. In others, things stay quiet even in a real emergency.

People are the same. Harvard psychologist Jerome Kagan ran an experiment where he showed babies unfamiliar sights and popped balloons near them. About 40% of the babies barely reacted at all.\[1]

* **Babies who barely reacted**: Their danger detector (the amygdala) tended to be less sensitive. As adults, they **tended to become "approach types"**: outgoing, confident, and always thinking about the reward before the risk.
* **Babies who reacted strongly**: Their danger detector tended to be more sensitive. As adults, they **tended to become "avoidance types"**: looking for mistakes and risks before thinking about the reward.

Approach types dive in, sometimes get in trouble, and learn fast.

Avoidance types, on the other hand, step back, watch, and try to steer clear of uncomfortable situations.

**That's reactivity. Think of it as the building's built-in "sensitivity setting."**

## 4. When it's time to renovate: flexibility

So are sensitive avoidance types always at a disadvantage? Here's the twist.

**When things suddenly change, avoidance types are actually more flexible.**

**The key is noradrenaline, that "security alert switch."**

When things become uncertain, this switch flips on for avoidance types and sends a strong signal to the brain: "Drop the old way and learn what fits the situation now." In fact, research shows that people who took a drug blocking noradrenaline kept clinging to past experience, even after the situation had changed, and couldn't adjust their behavior to fit.\[1]

* **Avoidance types**: **The alert switch flips on easily.** → They read the current situation to predict what's next, and when plans fall apart, they change direction quickly.
* **Approach types**: **The alert switch doesn't flip on as easily.** → They predict the future from the past ("This worked last time"), and when plans fall apart, they're more likely to get rattled.

Back to the building: avoidance types are buildings that renovate quickly to fit new needs, while approach types are buildings that rarely change their original layout.

That's **flexibility**. What really surprised me is that, contrary to what most people would assume, the book says avoidance types handle this kind of flexibility better than approach types.

## 5. Repairs that fit your building: adaptability

So here's what I really want to say:

**Each type struggles to change different habits, so each type needs to check different things.**

**If you're more of an approach type, check the habits you keep "just because they're familiar."**

The way you eat, work out, sleep, and deal with stress: are you doing it because it's actually good for you, or just because it's what you've always done? If you only ever use a building the way it was originally laid out, it's easy to miss the old pipes that need replacing.

**If you're more of an avoidance type, check the habits you keep "to avoid something."**

You may adapt well to change, but you're not great at changing habits you repeat to avoid loss or discomfort. Look for behaviors you keep even though you know they aren't helping you. It's like never going into a certain room because you're afraid the alarm might go off.

**And the prescription is the same for both types:**

1. ***Pick one action or change you've been putting off.***
2. ***Break it into a size you can handle, and set a start date.***
3. ***See discomfort and tension not as signs of failure, but as signs that you're taking on a challenge.*** *(positive thinking)*
4. ***Remember that getting used to something new takes time and patience.*** *(It's never easy.)*
5. ***Don't push too hard. Just keep heading in the right direction, little by little.*** *(Small steps so you don't give up.)*

That's your brain's **adaptability**.

You can't change the building you were born with, but you can always make repairs.

In the same way, we can't change the temperament we were born with, but we can always change, a little at a time.

Personally, this line from the book has stuck with me:

**"We have to learn to tolerate emotionally uncomfortable moments. Remember: good things can happen when we grow stronger."**

## 6. How to use your brain's systems when building a habit

Once I understood these systems, I started to see habit-building a little differently.

**1. The neon sign (dopamine) shines brighter when you're anticipating something**

Dopamine is known to be released more when we're **expecting** a reward than when we actually get it.\[2] That's why a small reward right after finishing a habit matters so much. Every morning after my workout, I log it in MOAVO Habits. The moment a dot appears on my calendar becomes a little neon sign that gets me out the door again the next morning.

**2. When the alert switch (noradrenaline) flips on, you're ready to learn**

That slight tension, awkwardness, and sense of pressure you feel when starting a new habit?

That's not a bad sign. It's your brain switching to alert mode and saying, "Time to learn something new."

It's your brain recognizing that it's time to change.

**3. Use the circuit breaker (GABA) and the thermostat (serotonin) to get through uncomfortable moments**

Most habits fall apart in the moments we can't tolerate the discomfort.

*Taking a deep breath, going for a walk, running, meditating, praying, journaling.*

If you have at least one way to calm yourself down, those moments get a lot easier to push through.

## 7. Take a look at your own building today

**Let's be honest. Are you closer to an approach type or an avoidance type?**

This isn't a label that sorts people into boxes. It's just "which way you tend to lean."

Everyone has both sides, and depending on what's going on in your life, that tendency keeps shifting.

Still, try writing these down at least once:

* **One habit you keep doing because it's familiar** *(e.g., scrolling social media as soon as you wake up, lying on the couch watching TV after work, still smoking when you meant to quit)*
* **One habit you're avoiding because it's uncomfortable** *(e.g., going out to exercise, putting off a health checkup, going to a new meetup, calling a family member)*

Then pick one and change it in a very small way.

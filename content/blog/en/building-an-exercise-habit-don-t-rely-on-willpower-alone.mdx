# Building an Exercise Habit? Don’t Rely on Willpower Alone

On your way home from work, the plan feels clear:&#x20;

**“Tonight, I’m going to exercise.”**

Then you walk through the door. The couch looks inviting. You sit down, check your phone, have a snack—and the window for a workout slips away. Soon, you’re asking yourself the familiar question:&#x20;

**“Why can’t I stick to anything?”**

Before making a bigger promise, look at what it takes to begin. **Is the action manageable with the time and energy you have? Is there something that prompts you to start?**

I explored those questions in my own life. My practice focused on morning exercise. The goal was a 20-minute workout, but the first step was much smaller: put on my shoes and go outside.



## 1.Three conditions for action: B=MAP

BJ Fogg, author of *Tiny Habits*, describes behavior through the model **B=MAP**. Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt must come together at the same moment for a behavior to occur.\[1]

This isn’t an equation for adding or multiplying numbers. It’s a framework for examining what might be missing when an action doesn’t happen.

| Element       | A question to ask about exercise                      |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Motivation | Do I want to do it?                                   |
| 2. Ability    | How easy is it to do with the time and energy I have? |
| 3. Prompt     | What reminds me to start now?                         |

You can want to exercise and still find a trip to the gym followed by an hour-long workout overwhelming after a tiring day. Putting on your shoes is a smaller action.

Putting on shoes does not replace a workout. It can, however, lower the hurdle to getting started. Leaving them ready by the door can reduce preparation and serve as a visual reminder when you see them.

**Motivation still matters. The useful shift is recognizing that increasing motivation isn’t the only thing you can work on. You can also make the action easier and arrange a prompt to begin.**



## 2. My approach: start with the shoes

To work toward 20 minutes of morning exercise, I set up a simple sequence:

1. ***Leave my exercise shoes ready by the front door.***
2. ***Make putting them on and going outside my first action after waking.***
3. ***Record what I did in MOAVO Habits after exercising.***
4. ***Receive encouragement and feedback from AVO, the app’s AI coach.***

This was my own adaptation of Fogg’s approach. Journaling and AI feedback were elements I added; they are not additional components of B=MAP.

The distinction that mattered was between **the goal and the starting action**. Exercise was the goal. At the moment of starting, I focused on getting my shoes on and stepping outside.



## 3. What my 149-day record showed

When I wrote about this experience on October 1, 2026, my personal tally was:

| Measure                               | My tally at the time                      |
| ------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Observation period                    | 149 days                                  |
| Days I counted as completing exercise | 133                                       |
| Completion rate                       | 89.3%                                     |
| Longest consecutive streak            | 46 days                                   |
| Running distance: starting vs. recent | 800 meters → 3\~4 kilometers(2\~2.5 mile) |
| Push-ups: starting vs. recent         | 20 → 70\~100 ea                           |

The running distances and push-up counts compare my starting level with recent sessions at the time. They are not daily averages.

What stands out to me is more than the longest streak. I did not complete exercise on all 149 days, yet the record preserved the exercise I had done across that period. It also gave me something concrete to consult when comparing where I started with where I was.

These numbers do not establish that one particular method caused the change. This was one person’s self-reported experience, with no control group. Preparation, a smaller starting action, repeated exercise, journaling, and feedback all happened together. I cannot separate their individual contributions.

For me, the value is having a record of **what I tried and how I continued**, rather than proof that the same approach will work for everyone.



## 4. A short note can tell you more than a checkmark

A checkmark shows that you did something. A sentence can preserve some of the circumstances around it.

For example, you might write the following. These are illustrative examples, not quotations from my journal:

* *“Woke up late, so I went outside for a short walk instead of a longer run.”*
* *“Spent too long looking for workout clothes. I’ll set them out tonight.”*
* *“Coming back after a break felt difficult, so I kept today’s session short.”*

Later, you can ask both “How often did I do it?” and “What made starting easier?” Your notes won’t establish cause and effect, but they can suggest something worth adjusting next time.



## 5. Choose one small action and write one line

Pick something you want to do more consistently. Make the first action small: open a book, or put on your exercise shoes. Decide when you’ll do it, too.

Afterward, write one sentence about **what you did and what made it easy or difficult**. If you didn’t do it, you can record that and the circumstances as well. After a few days, look back and choose one helpful condition to keep or one obstacle to change.

You don’t need to document your whole life. Start with one behavior that matters to you.

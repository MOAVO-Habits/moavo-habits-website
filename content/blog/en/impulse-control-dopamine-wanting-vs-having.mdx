# How to Control Impulses: Dopamine Is About the Thrill of Wanting, Not the Joy of Having

It's 11 p.m. You're lying in bed, and you open a shopping app.

You don't really need anything. But as you scroll, items start landing in your cart, one by one. The moment you hit "Place Order," you feel great. And yet, strangely, when you tear open the box a few days later, that excitement is nowhere to be found.

Late-night snacking works the same way.

I'd put on some weight, so I made a rule: no eating after 8:30 p.m. But on days when work piles up and I've squeezed out every last drop of energy, I come home and, almost without thinking, open a delivery app and order something high-calorie, like fried chicken or pizza.

For me, the biggest one was cigarettes.

Over 27 years, I quit five times. And five times, I started again.

So why do we keep making impulsive choices, even when we know better?

Today, I want to talk about how to handle the impulses in our lives.

## 1. Impulses Aren't the Enemy

First, let's clear up a common misunderstanding about impulses.

An impulse isn't bad in itself. Put simply, an impulse is "a thought, tangled up with emotion, that wants a decision to be made."\[1]

"I want to try this." "I want to eat that right now." "I want to go talk to that person."

These are the very feelings that push us to try new things, meet new people, and actually enjoy life.

The problem isn't the impulse. The problem is not knowing when to say yes to it and when to say no.

Interestingly, people lean in different directions when it comes to handling impulses.

* **Some people say yes too easily:** they get swept up in shopping, late-night snacks, or the latest new hobby.
* **Some people say no too strictly:** they hold back even from things they want to do and are allowed to enjoy, and end up boxing themselves in.

## 2. The Truth About Dopamine: We Don't Want to *Have*, We Want to *Anticipate*

Here's a fascinating story from the book *Override* by Connell Cowan and David Kipper.

There was a woman in the U.S. who couldn't stop buying wallets. She was so fixated on them that whenever she had a free moment, she'd buy another one online. Realizing she had a problem, she started seeing a therapist. The therapist asked her to take every wallet she owned, lay them all out on her bed, and stick a Post-it note on each one with the last date she'd used it.

The wallets covered the entire bed: 41 of them. But when she checked the dates on the Post-its, she had actually used only three in the past six months.\[1]

Here's what she realized:

The rush she felt when buying a wallet was never the joy of *owning* it.

It was the anticipation of looking at a wallet on her phone screen and imagining, "How would it feel to have this?" That, she said, was the whole thing.

In fact, research suggests dopamine has less to do with enjoying and savoring things,

and much more to do with anticipating, dreaming, and planning.\[2]

Once you know this, impulses start to look a little different.

That good feeling when you hit "Place Order" doesn't come from finally owning the thing. It comes from the burst of anticipation in your brain. That's why the excitement is gone by the time you open the box. You already spent it. One thing she said has stuck with me:

"I can imagine how it would feel to have the wallet on the screen. But I don't need to actually buy it. I know now that I'd probably never use it."

With her therapist's help, she learned to separate anticipation from ownership, and for the first time, she was able to manage the impulse.

## 3. Same Self-Control, So Why Is It Easier for Some People?

It's the night before an exam, and a friend asks if you want to play one round of a video game. Do you hold out and study, or play just one round?

Most of us have probably been there at least once.

But in the exact same situation, the math in each person's head is different.

|                                 | Drawn to rewards (Swords)                         | Driven to avoid losses (Shields)                             |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| What they see when holding back | The fun they're missing (games, Netflix, a date)  | The loss they're avoiding (the risk of bombing the exam)     |
| The trap they fall into         | Lose their grip, turn on the TV, and start gaming | Avoid what's uncomfortable and mistake it for "self-control" |

*Override* calls the first group **Dopamine Types ("Swords")** and the second **Serotonin Types ("Shields")**. Think of these less as labels and more as the direction each of us tends to lean.\[1]

The trap worth focusing on here is the Shield's.

People who avoid losses are good at holding back.

The problem is that sometimes they mistake *avoiding something uncomfortable* for *patiently waiting.*

"I'm not ready yet." "Now's not the right time."

Behind these reasonable-sounding excuses, there's often just a simple dislike of discomfort.

Swords, on the other hand, are great at starting. But the moment things get a little boring, they jump to the next shiny thing. That's how they end up with a pile of projects that got started but never finished.

Honestly, I think I'm a bit of both. Which way do you lean?

## 4. What the Marshmallow Test Didn't Tell Us

Let's talk about delayed gratification.

Stanford psychologist Walter Mischel placed a marshmallow in front of preschoolers and told them that if they could wait 15 minutes without eating it, they'd get a second one. The famous finding was that the kids who waited longer went on to get better test scores and have fewer behavioral problems.

But there's more to the story.

Later researchers argued that family background needed to be taken into account. When a follow-up study controlled for factors like household income and home environment, the link became much weaker.\[3]

If you're a kid from a home where you're often hungry and food is always scarce, grabbing the marshmallow right away might actually be the natural choice, because "if I don't eat it now, it might disappear." When you're hungry and going without today, can you really trust a promise of more later? It makes sense to grab what's in front of you first.

I actually found comfort in this part.

Giving in to an impulse isn't always about weak willpower.

Your situation, and how exhausted you are, play a part too.

That doesn't mean delayed gratification doesn't matter, or that the original research was completely wrong. Whatever the debate among researchers, self-control and delayed gratification are still important skills to build.\[1] But you can't build them just by telling yourself to "tough it out." What we need to remember is that these skills interact with each person's environment and circumstances.

## 5. The Solution: Focus on the Process, Not the Outcome

So what do we do?

"Think about the process, not the goal."

The book tells the story of a man who had wanted to learn to dance for a long time. But he didn't want anyone to see him stumble, so he avoided dancing at every party. He kept meaning to sign up for lessons, and he always found an excuse. The real reason was that signing up itself felt embarrassing and uncomfortable. But when he finally enrolled and actually danced, he gained something far more valuable than dance skills:

The confidence and belief that "I can actually do something I thought I never could."\[1]

When we only look at the outcome, impulses tend to win. A distant goal like "someday I'll be a good dancer" can't beat tonight's discomfort. But when you focus on the process, everything changes. I went to class today. I followed one move today. Each of these small steps becomes a Small Win, proof that "I'm someone who can do this."

## 6. How to Make the Process Visible: Habit Tracking

The problem is that the process of getting to an outcome is hard to see.

Impulses are always vivid. The item in your cart. The food photo in the delivery app. The cigarette between your fingers.

But the one time you held back today, or the small thing you got done? We just breeze right past it.

And when the process stays invisible, it's hard to beat the impulse right in front of you.

That's why I believe tracking matters.

Tracking does three things.

**First, it shows you the moment the impulse hits.**

When I started logging my smoking, I found that my eight cigarettes a day always came from the same places, the same times, and the same routines. Before work. After meals. Right before heading home. Impulses don't strike at random. They come from specific settings and behavior patterns. You have to know those scenes before you can prepare for them.

**Second, it lets you compare what you expected with what actually happened.**

Just like laying 41 wallets out on a bed, tracking lets you check, "Did I really need that?" On a day you make an impulse buy, write down something like, "Saw something I loved and bought it. I'm so excited." Then read it again a week later. You'll probably see exactly where that excitement went.

**Third, it turns the process itself into a small reward.**

If dopamine responds to anticipation, you can hang that anticipation on the process instead of the impulse.

"I held back today! I did it today!" A dot lands on the calendar,

and the anticipation of keeping that streak going tomorrow, all on its own, slowly pushes impulsive choices and actions aside.

So how should you write it down? It doesn't have to be fancy.

| When                                   | What pulled at me          | What I did                                                     |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 11 p.m., in bed                        | Sneakers in a shopping app | Left them in the cart and decided to look again tomorrow       |
| Right after work, totally drained      | Late-night takeout         | Drank a glass of water, held out for 10 minutes, and felt fine |
| Walking back to the office after lunch | A cigarette                | Smoked one. Tomorrow I'll try a different route                |

Write down the days you slip, too. Those are actually the most valuable material.
